Your B2B client's YouTube channel is a parking lot

Published
12 May 2026
Reading time
3 min
Written by
Saurabh Tripathi

Most of the B2B clients I have worked with this year have a YouTube channel. Almost none of them are using it. They are decorating it. There is a difference, and the buyer behavior data from the last twelve months has made the gap embarrassing.

Here is what I mean. The default B2B move is to treat YouTube the way you would treat a billboard or a brand TV spot. The client commissions a polished two-minute company video, uploads it, writes the description the CEO likes, and measures the channel by view count and "the video looks great." The channel becomes a parking lot for sales reels, conference recordings, and the occasional founder interview.

The thing is, that is not what YouTube is for the buyer.

The 6sense 2025 B2B Buyer Experience Report, published in November and based on responses from over 4,000 buyers across three regions, found that the balance between independent buyer research and seller engagement has shifted from 70/30 to 60/40 in a single year. 94 percent of buying groups already have a preferred vendor ranked before the first sales conversation. 77 percent of those groups end up buying from the vendor they had already favored before they reached out.

That is the game. The work is being done before anyone fills out a contact form. The buyer is going to YouTube, typing a specific question into the search bar, and watching whatever answers it well.

Your B2B client is almost never showing up on those searches. Their channel has a corporate explainer with 8,000 views from a paid campaign, no chapters, a transcript that does not answer a real question, and a thumbnail that says "We Are XYZ Corp." They are not in the buyer's consideration set because they never showed up where the buyer was looking.

The reason most B2B clients keep treating YouTube as a brand surface is that nobody on their team owns the search side of it. The website has SEO. The Google Ads account has a paid search lead. The YouTube channel has whoever happens to make videos, usually with no input from the people who know which queries decide pipeline. That gap is the whole opportunity.

The objection I hear most from marketing leads is that B2B videos do not get views, so why bother. The math runs the other way. A query that gets 800 searches a month is uncontested if your video is the third result. 800 searches a month, half of them from people already inside a buying group, is more pipeline than a brand video with 80,000 views from a paid push. Views are the wrong metric for this channel. Surface area on the right queries is the right metric.

What changed in 2025 makes this worse, or better, depending on which side of it you are on. YouTube's algorithm now leans on AI models including Gemini to read on-screen elements, transcripts, and topic structure, not just titles and tags. Long-form discoverability now leans on whether the video is structured around answers, not narrative arcs. The agencies still optimizing for "engagement" and "watch time" alone are competing on the wrong axis with people who understand search intent better than they do.

What I would do for a B2B client who actually wants to use the channel is much smaller and less glamorous than what most of them ask for. Pull 20 queries that buyers in their category actually type. Most will have under a thousand monthly searches each. That is the point. Make one short video for each query, plainly titled, with chapters and a description that reads like a useful answer rather than marketing copy. Skip the brand intro. Then watch what shows up over six months.

Where do those 20 queries come from. Three places, mostly. The questions your sales team hears on first calls, written down verbatim. The People Also Ask box on Google for your category and your competitors. The autocomplete bar in YouTube's own search when you type the seed terms a buyer would. Spend an afternoon on those three and you will have more than 20.

None of that requires a production budget. It requires somebody on the team who can think in queries instead of campaigns, and a willingness to publish a video that does not have the company's logo on screen for the first eight seconds.

The B2B clients who win the next two years on YouTube are not going to be the ones with the best production. They will be the ones who treated the channel like it was actually search.
